# How a round works, in plain language

You bring the council something: a company you sit on the board of, an idea you are tempted by, a deal on the table, a decision at home, an initiative at work, a question you cannot settle. That is an **effort**. The council you bring it to is a **bench**: the hat you are wearing, with the seats that hat needs.

A **round** is one full pass of the council on that effort. It always runs in the same order:

1. **Intake and the plan.** In chat, the session asks you one batch of questions: what the thing is, the case for it as its proponents make it, what you believe that should be tested, what worries you, which documents you can share. It writes a brief. Then it plans the run with you: your dollar ceiling, which seats sit, which model each one uses, and what it should cost, on one screen. Nothing runs until you say go.
2. **Roster.** The chair reads the bench's seats and rates how relevant each one is to this effort. Seats that have nothing to say sit out.
3. **Analysts** (three at a time, blind to each other). Each establishes facts in its lane: the numbers, the market and the rivals, the rules, how it actually works, what the buyer is really buying, what precedent says, what the evidence says, what the real options are. Every fact gets an evidence record with a source. Everything they expected to find and could not becomes a recorded gap.
4. **Conflict register.** The chair lines up the facts and logs every place two sources disagree. Nothing is quietly resolved.
5. **Stakeholders** (in parallel). Each speaks from a seat, not from yours: the customer, the user, the employee, the partner, the regulator, the investor, the person who has to agree with you. Each answers three questions: how does this land with me, what would make me back it or walk, what could I do that hurts it. Sentiment has to cite something.
6. **Red team.** An adversary with eighteen months and real resources explains how it would make the effort fail. A pre-mortem writes the retrospective from the end of the horizon. Both name the earliest signals you would see.
7. **Rebuttal.** Every stakeholder gets one pass to defend or revise its view against the others and the red team. One pass, no chains.
8. **Answer pass.** Open challenges go back to the analyst best placed to answer them.
9. **Evidence auditor.** Checks every headline's chain back to its sources, demotes what does not hold, and names the single weakest piece of evidence the score rests on.
10. **Synthesis.** The chair scores the bench's drivers from cited findings, writes the verdict, tiers every open item by how much it matters, and produces the assessment, the dashboard, and **what the council needs from you**.

That last list is the hinge. The council does not stop because it ran out of things to say; it stops because the rest depends on things only you can supply: a document, a decision, an answer. You answer what you can, when you can, and start the **next round**. The council carries every piece of evidence, every open conflict and every unanswered request forward, re-runs only the seats your answers touch, and tells you what moved and why. Round after round, for as long as the effort lives.

## What you get after each round
- **The score**, 0 to 100 with a confidence band, against the question your bench asks, and the three things that moved it.
- **The assessment**, a written document in a neutral voice that you could forward unedited.
- **The dashboard**, one HTML file with an executive view, a narrative view for sharing, and an audit view where every ID is clickable back to its evidence.
- **The requests list**, numbered and tiered, Tier 0 first.
- **The ledger**, the full record behind the dashboard.
- **The cost receipt**: the estimate against what the run actually cost, by step, and why any step went over.

## When the council stops to ask
Rarely, the council finds something that changes the question itself: a fact the whole round rests on turns out to be false, the goal cannot be reached as framed, or news flips the decision. Then it stops and asks you how to go on instead of finishing an answer to a question nobody is asking any more. Ordinary bad news is not that; it goes in the assessment.

## What the council will not do
- Invent a fact to keep moving. It records a gap instead.
- Let a headline through without corroboration. Only you can waive that, on the record.
- Read one effort's documents while working on another.
- Address you as "you" in an output, so the output can go to anyone.
